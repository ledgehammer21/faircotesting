Why fall is planting season in the low desert

Cooler air and warm soil give new trees and shrubs a full season to root before summer heat arrives.

By the Fairco Plant Health Care team · 3 min read

Most people plant in spring, when nurseries fill up and the weather turns pleasant. In the low desert, that timing works against the plant. A tree set in the ground in April has a few weeks before temperatures pass 100 degrees, and its roots haven't spread far enough to replace the water it loses through its leaves.

Fall reverses that. From October into early spring, the air cools while the soil holds the warmth of summer. Roots keep growing in warm soil, and the plant above ground needs less water to stay healthy. By the time the heat returns, a fall planting has had five or six months to establish.

Start with the right plant

Desert-adapted species handle the heat and alkaline soil here with less help. Palo verde, mesquite, ironwood and desert willow are reliable shade trees, and most native shrubs and grasses do well when planted in fall. Check the mature size against the space before you choose. A tree that outgrows its spot will need heavy pruning for the rest of its life.

Dig wide, plant at grade

The planting hole should be two to three times as wide as the root ball and no deeper than it. Set the plant so the root flare, where the trunk widens into the roots, sits at or slightly above the surrounding soil. Planting too deep is one of the most common reasons young trees decline. Backfill with the native soil you removed, since heavy amendments can keep roots from growing out into the ground around them.

Water deeply, then taper

New plantings need regular, deep watering while their roots spread. Each cycle should soak the full root ball and the soil around it, then let it dry slightly before the next. Over the first year the schedule stretches out as the roots extend. Shallow, frequent watering keeps roots near the surface, where they dry out quickly in summer.

What to watch during the first year

  • Afternoon wilting or leaf scorch, which can mean the root ball is drying out
  • Yellowing leaves over soggy soil, a sign of too much water
  • Stakes or ties rubbing the bark. Remove stakes once the tree stands on its own
  • Mulch piled against the trunk. Keep it a few inches back

Let the schedule follow the season

A new planting's water needs change every few months. On the properties we manage, irrigation for new plantings is adjusted by season and checked in the field, so young plants get enough water to establish while the mature landscape around them stays on its own schedule.
